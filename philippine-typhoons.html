In Harm's Way

Mapping the Philippines' storm–poverty trap: 67 years of typhoon tracks, crossed with official poverty statistics, reveal where disaster keeps development from taking hold — and who is being left behind.
An independent data story · July 2026 · built from JMA best-track data, PSA poverty statistics, and open boundary data

No country on Earth is hit by more tropical cyclones than the Philippines. Roughly twenty storms enter the Philippine Area of Responsibility every year, and eight or nine cross the archipelago itself. Each one arrives with the same question attached: who ends up paying for it — and for how long?

Inspired by the Asian Development Bank and Thinking Machines' work mapping digital poverty, this story applies the same method to a different divide. We took the Japan Meteorological Agency's complete best-track archive — the position and intensity of every Western Pacific tropical cyclone since 1951 — and measured, for each of the country's 82 provinces (plus Metro Manila), how often a storm of tropical-storm strength or stronger passed within 100 km. We then crossed that exposure with the Philippine Statistics Authority's official poverty incidence for 2018, 2021, and 2023, and with census population counts. Everything in this story is computed from open data; methods and caveats are at the end.

Typhoon exposure is a lottery of geography — some provinces face one storm a year, others one a century.

The exposure map is dramatic. Cagayan, at Luzon's northeastern tip, saw 176 storm passages in 67 seasons — about 26 per decade, or between two and three every year. The whole of the country's eastern seaboard, from Batanes down through Bicol to Eastern Samar, sits in the steady lane of the Pacific storm track.

Slide south and the lottery flips. Sulu recorded zero tropical-storm-strength passages within 100 km in the entire 67-year archive; Basilan and Tawi-Tawi recorded one each. Most of southern and western Mindanao faces a storm less than once a decade. When one does arrive — Sendong in 2011, Pablo in 2012, Odette in 2021 — it strikes communities with little built memory of typhoons, which is its own kind of danger.

Cross the storm map with the poverty map, and the country splits into three.

Here is the surprise: nationally, storms and poverty do not line up. The correlation between a province's typhoon exposure and its 2023 poverty rate is actually negative (r = −0.42) — because the country's deepest poverty sits in the most sheltered corner. The Bangsamoro provinces, scarred by decades of conflict rather than wind, are poor and nearly storm-free. Meanwhile the most-hammered provinces of Northern Luzon — Cagayan, the Ilocos coast — are middling-to-prosperous farm economies.

The dark-blue provinces — poor and battered — form a clear corridor: Bicol (Sorsogon, Camarines Sur, Catanduanes, Albay, Masbate), the Samar-Leyte island group, and the Mimaropa islands (the Mindoros, Romblon, Palawan). These are the places where the Pacific's storm lane and the poverty map overlap. Twenty storm-belt provinces have poverty above the national rate; together they are home to 25.8 million people.

The trap shows up in the trend: while the sheltered south escaped poverty, the storm belt stood still.

Between 2018 and 2023 the national family poverty rate fell from 12.1% to 10.9%, and the biggest declines in the country's history were recorded in Mindanao — BARMM's rate fell by 24 percentage points. Group the provinces by storm exposure, weight by population, and the pattern is stark:

Catanduanes — the small island that meets more typhoons head-on than almost anywhere in the country — saw family poverty jump from 14.4% to 23.5% in five years, even as the nation grew less poor.

The timing is not mysterious. In a single quarter of 2020, Catanduanes took a direct hit from Super Typhoon Rolly (Goni) — at landfall one of the strongest storms ever recorded anywhere — followed two weeks later by Typhoon Ulysses (Vamco). The 2021 poverty survey caught the aftermath, and by 2023 the island had not recovered its 2018 ground; in 2024 it was struck again, by Kristine (Trami). Sorsogon (+8.8 pp), Palawan (+7.6), Oriental Mindoro (+6.8, hit by the double landfalls of 2020–21 and the Odette track) and Occidental Mindoro (+4.5) tell the same story. Research has long backed the mechanism: economists Anttila-Hughes and Hsiang, studying Philippine household data, found that a typhoon's economic losses run to many times its visible damage, depressing household incomes and spending for years after landfall.

At the margins: 18 million Filipinos live where poverty is above the national line, storms keep coming — and the trend points the wrong way.

Note what this table is not saying: it is not saying typhoons are the country's main cause of poverty — Basilan, Sulu and Maguindanao remind us that conflict and governance matter at least as much. What it shows is a different failure mode: in the storm belt, poverty is not deep so much as it is stuck. Provinces elsewhere ride the national growth wave out of poverty; here, every few years, a storm knocks the ladder over. The most exposed provinces that stayed prosperous — Cagayan, the Ilocos coast — are precisely the ones with generations of adaptation: typhoon-hardened crops and calendars, concrete housing stock, dense state presence. The trap closes where high exposure meets thin buffers: small island economies dependent on coconut, fishing, and abaca, where one landfall can erase a decade of gains.

What would it take to break the trap?

The policy toolkit exists; the analysis says where to aim it. First, pre-position resilience money in the corridor, not after the storm. The provinces in the table above are identifiable in advance — disaster risk financing, parametric insurance, and forecast-based cash transfers (releasing funds when a track forecast crosses a province, before landfall) can reach them before losses compound into poverty. Second, treat recovery as a development program, not an emergency. The Catanduanes and Mindoro numbers show impacts persisting through two survey rounds; reconstruction windows measured in months will keep losing to storms that arrive on a faster cycle. Third, harden the escape routes out of agriculture. Where livelihoods are wind-proof — services, remittances, manufacturing — the same storms cause loss but not poverty; the Ilocos coast is the proof. And fourth, watch the sheltered south's blind spot: as the climate shifts storm tracks, provinces with no typhoon memory — where our maps show one storm per generation — suffer catastrophically when the odds break, as Sendong, Pablo, and Odette each proved.

The Philippines cannot choose its geography. But the difference between Cagayan and Catanduanes — between exposure that a society has grown strong around and exposure that keeps knocking it down — is not geography. It is investment, arriving early enough, in the places the data can already name.

Methods & caveats

  • Exposure is the count of distinct tropical cyclones at tropical-storm strength or higher (JMA grades TS, STS, TY, and the pre-1977 "TS-or-higher" class) whose best-track line passed within 100 km of a province's boundary, from the Japan Meteorological Agency RSMC-Tokyo best-track archive, seasons 1951–2017 (as parsed in the open Typhoon-viewer mirror), expressed per decade. The 100 km buffer is a standard proxy for damaging-wind radius; results are qualitatively stable at 50–150 km. Long-run climatology is stable, so the 2018–2025 storms (Rolly, Ulysses, Odette, Egay, Kristine…) do not change the exposure ranking — but they are exactly the events the poverty trend reflects.
  • Poverty is the PSA's official poverty incidence among families by province for 2018, 2021, and 2023 (2023 Full-Year Official Poverty Statistics, Table 1). Province-level estimates carry sampling error (PSA publishes coefficients of variation); single-province swings — notably Sulu's 62-point drop, which PSA itself flags for measurement changes — should be read with care. Our class-level and population-weighted results do not depend on any single province.
  • Population and boundaries: PSA-based provincial population series (via Wikipedia's provinces table, 2024 projections; Metro Manila at its 2020 census count) and PSGC 2023 administrative boundaries from faeldon/philippines-json-maps. Metro Manila is analyzed as one unit; the City of Isabela is counted with Basilan.
  • Association, not proof. This is a descriptive analysis. Storm-belt provinces differ from sheltered ones in many ways (island size, market access, conflict history), and 2021 poverty figures also carry COVID-19 effects that struck regions unevenly. The causal literature (e.g., Anttila-Hughes & Hsiang on Philippine household income after typhoons) supports the mechanism our descriptive pattern suggests.
